Representation is not the same as power

Youth influence has to be more than a picture in a campaign.

Youthwashing happens when young people are shown, quoted or used as symbols without being able to influence the decisions behind the facade.

Recognise the pattern See the method

Definition

What is youthwashing?

Youthwashing is when companies, public authorities or organisations use young people's language, aesthetics or engagement to appear modern and responsive, without sharing real responsibility, budget or decision-making power.

Visibility can be valuable, but it is not enough. The question is whether young people only get to be the public face, or whether their perspective actually changes direction, priorities and results.

Quick diagnosis

Three warning signs

01

Young people are seen but do not decide

They appear in pictures, panel discussions and campaigns but have no influence over decisions, budget and strategy.

02

Radical language, unchanged structure

The organisation talks about the future and participation, but the same people still hold all the power.

03

Engagement becomes branding

Young people's causes are packaged as image without long-term support for the work already under way.

From symbol to influence

Four levels of participation

  1. Decoration Young people are used to create feeling, energy or credibility.
  2. Consultation Young people may give their views, but the framework is already decided.
  3. Co-creation Young people are involved early and influence goals, content and priorities.
  4. Decision-making power Young people have a mandate, resources and the ability to stop or change decisions.

How the placement is worked out

Decision-making power requires yes on mandate and resources. Co-creation requires yes on timing and at least partly on mandate. Consultation means young people get to give their views in some form. Each ticked warning sign moves the placement one step down. It is a rough guide, not an assessment.

Five questions before a campaign launches

  1. Which young people were involved before the idea was finished?
  2. Which decisions can they actually influence?
  3. Is there pay, time and support for their work?
  4. Are critical voices welcome even when they disturb the message?
  5. What happens after the campaign when the attention fades?

The questions are also available as a printable worksheet for workshops, and as constructed examples from a school, an association, a company and a municipality.
